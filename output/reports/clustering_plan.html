On the public data, the 50 km radius never binds. Amazon stops sit about 160 m from their 4th-nearest neighbour, so a 50 km limit is roughly 300× too loose to matter.

The radius only cuts in once it is less than about 4× that spacing. That points to regional bulky delivery, probably Ryry's setting. Ryry's data, or a few numbers about it, now decides whether the paper exists.

What's going on, explained simply

Imagine a furniture shop that has to deliver sofas and fridges to hundreds of homes tomorrow. It wants to send as few trucks as possible, and every truck trip has to follow four rules. That's the whole puzzle.

1The puzzle

Each dot is a home waiting for something big. Each circle is one truck trip. The rules for every trip:

  1. The truck stops at 5 homes at most.
  2. Everything has to fit in the truck.
  3. Every home must be within 50 km of the trip's middle.
  4. Every home gets exactly one visit.

Fewer circles means fewer trucks, drivers and fuel.

2The floor: a score nobody can beat

Some limits can't be beaten. With 30 homes and 5 stops per truck, you need at least 6 trucks. Space works the same way: if all the furniture together fills 7 trucks, you need at least 7. The bigger of those two numbers is the floor. We score every method by how far above the floor it lands.

3Ryry's tool today

It never breaks a rule, which is great. But it uses about 11.5% more trucks, roughly 112 where 100 might do. It also makes some trips more spread out than they need to be. Ryry asked: can an AI help pick better trips while keeping all four rules?

4This week's test: real Amazon addresses

We tested on real delivery addresses from Amazon in five US cities (1,066 depot-days). We found two surprises:

  • The homes are packed close together. A home's 4th-closest neighbour is only about 160 m away, about two football fields. The 50 km rule is around 300 times bigger, so it never matters. It's like a rule saying "everyone in one car must live in the same city" when they all live on one street.
  • The parcels are tiny. A whole day's parcels fit in about 1.5 trucks, but the 5-stop rule alone needs about 62. So the space rule never matters either.

With neither rule biting, a simple method already hits the floor exactly: 0% extra trucks. On this data, a clever method has nothing left to fix.

5Making the puzzle hard on purpose

So we shrank the circle and blew the parcels up to sofa size, to find where trucks get wasted. The gap map shows the answer: the circle rule only starts to hurt once it's smaller than about 4 neighbour-distances. For a 50 km rule, that means homes about 12–50 km apart, like delivering furniture across the countryside. That's probably Ryry's world, which is why Ryry's data matters so much.

Oct 28 proposed

The idea: shortlist, then solve exactly

An AI (GNN) shortlists good teammates for each home, and an exact solver picks the trips from the shortlist. Ryry's own trips stay in as a backup, so the answer can never be worse than today's.

Why: an AI alone can break rules, and an exact solver alone gets slow on big days. Closest prior work: Spieckermann, Minner & Schiffer, Transportation Science 2025.

Self-roast, with measured numbers

  1. deadThe Belgium set is useless here. Coordinates sit on a unitless 0–2,000 grid, so "50 km" means nothing. Demand is 0–3 against capacity 100, so volume can never bind with 5 stops.
  2. deadOn Amazon, the radius never binds. The median distance to the 4th-nearest stop (call it d4) is 0.16 km. A typical day's stops all lie within 8.2 km of the centre (the most spread-out day: 54.5 km).
  3. deadOn Amazon, raw volume never binds either. A day needs about 62 clusters for the 5-stop rule but only about 1.5 truckloads by volume. A simple greedy hits the lower bound exactly, so there is nothing to improve.
  4. weakScaling volumes up to "bulky" opens a ~5% gap, but it isn't geographic. The gap is the same at 3 km and at 50 km, so it is a packing problem. A GNN that learns who is near whom can't help with it. Whether the gap is real or just a weak bound is still unknown.
  5. dead"30k customers" is gone. It came only from Belgium. Amazon's depot-days are samples of about 2–3 routes (median 308 stops, max 3,453), not full days.
  6. wrong"Within Y% of optimal" was not a valid claim. Solving over a pruned candidate set gives no lower bound. Only the simple bound, max(⌈n/5⌉, ⌈total volume ÷ capacity⌉), or column generation on small cases counts.
  7. riskThe novelty is incremental over Spieckermann, Minner & Schiffer (Transportation Science 2025).
  8. deadLoggi Brazil benchmark (7k–32k deliveries, MIT license): both the repo and the download return 404.
  9. opsThe AWS GPU is billing for nothing. The project also has no version control, and another agent edited the same files at the same time yesterday.

How to read it

  • The radius hurts only when it is tight compared with customer spacing (the top row falls from 32% to 0% left to right). For a 50 km limit, that means customers whose 4 nearest neighbours are about 12–50 km away: regional bulky delivery.
  • High volume pressure opens gaps at any radius. Some of that may be a weak lower bound rather than a real gap. Exact solves (Plan v2, step 1) will tell.
  • This map is the honest public benchmark. It uses real geography and two scale-free knobs. Ryry's data becomes one point on it, located by two numbers.

The greedy picks seeds farthest from the day's centre first, then adds the nearest unassigned stops while all four rules hold; a self-check confirms each run's output. Scaled volumes put the median stop at pressure × capacity ÷ 5. At pressure 1.0, 6% of stops exceeded a truck and were capped at capacity for this check.

Plan v2

1

Exact solves on small days

Run CP-SAT at every cell of the map. This splits each gap into "the greedy is bad" and "the bound is weak", and shows where real room exists. CPU only, 1–2 days.

2

Decision gate, set in advance

Run the exact solver on candidates from plain nearest-neighbour pruning, with no learning.

Within ~1% of the best, in minutes, everywhere
No GNN. The paper becomes exact clustering with a never-worse guarantee, plus the map (target: an operations-research journal).
Falls short, or too slow at large sizes
The GNN scores the candidates. It goes in only if it beats nearest-neighbour pruning at the same candidate budget.
